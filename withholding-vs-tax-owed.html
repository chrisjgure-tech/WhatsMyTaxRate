Why Your Paycheck Withholding Is Almost Never Equal to Your Actual Tax Bill

In 2024, the IRS issued 105 million refunds averaging $3,138 — evidence that over-withholding is the norm, not the exception. Here is why the two numbers almost never match, what pushes them apart, and how to close the gap.

What Withholding Actually Is (and What It Isn't)

Withholding is not your tax bill. It is an estimate your employer makes and sends to the IRS on your behalf, a little from each paycheck throughout the year. Think of it as a running prepayment toward the annual total you will owe when you file in April.

Your employer does not know your full tax picture. They do not know whether you have a second job, a working spouse, freelance income, or large deductions. All they have is the information on your Form W-4 and a set of IRS withholding tables. Those tables do their best — but they are built around a simple default: one income, standard deduction, no credits. Any time your situation is more complicated than that default, the estimate will be off.

The key distinction

Withholding is what your employer sends in. Tax owed is what you actually owe after your income, deductions, and credits are all accounted for. These two numbers settle against each other when you file. Too much withheld → refund. Too little withheld → balance due.

The 3 Reasons You Almost Always Over-Withhold

1. The tables assume you earned that amount all year

Each paycheck, the IRS tables annualize your wages as if you earned that much every pay period for the full year. If you started a new job in July, the tables see a full-year salary and withhold accordingly — even though you only worked half the year. Result: too much comes out, and you get it back in spring.

2. The tables assume you only take the standard deduction

If you contribute to a traditional 401(k), HSA, or deductible IRA, those contributions lower your taxable income — but withholding tables do not automatically account for them unless you enter them on Form W-4 Step 4(b). Every dollar you shelter that the tables do not know about is a dollar you over-withhold on.

3. Your W-4 is out of date

Most people fill out a W-4 when they start a job and never touch it again. Life changes — marriage, a child, buying a home — all change your tax picture. An old W-4 calibrated to a different life keeps withholding at the wrong level until you update it.

The 3 Reasons You Can End Up Under-Withheld

1. Multiple income sources

Each employer withholds as if that job is your only income. If you have two W-2 jobs, or a W-2 job and freelance 1099 income, each source withholds at too low a rate. The combined income pushes you into a higher bracket, and not enough has been set aside. This is one of the most common causes of a surprise balance due.

2. Two-earner households

When both spouses work and file jointly, your combined income is taxed in the same brackets as a single high earner. But each employer only knows their own payroll. Unless both W-4s account for the other spouse's income (using Step 2 on the form), each employer withholds as if that paycheck were the household's only income — and the shortfall adds up.

3. Mid-year income changes

A raise, a large bonus, vested stock options, or the sale of an investment can all push your year-end income significantly above what your withholding was sized for. Bonuses in particular are often withheld at a flat 22% supplemental rate, which may be lower than your actual marginal rate once the bonus is stacked on top of your salary.

How to Use IRS Form W-4 to Fix Your Withholding

Form W-4 is the lever. You give it to your employer, and they adjust how much they withhold each pay period starting with the next cycle. Here is how to use it:

Step 1 — Use the IRS Withholding Estimator first

Before touching the form, run the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov/W4app. It asks for your pay stubs, other income, expected deductions, and any credits, then tells you exactly what to enter on each line. It takes about 15 minutes and removes the guesswork entirely.

Step 2 — Fill in Step 2 if you have multiple jobs or a working spouse

Check the box in Step 2(c) if you have two jobs with similar pay, or use the IRS estimator to calculate a more precise extra withholding amount to enter in Step 4(c). This is the single most impactful fix for under-withholding in two-earner households.

Step 3 — Account for deductions in Step 4(b)

If you expect to itemize, or if you have above-the-line deductions like a large 401(k) or HSA contribution that are not already reflected in payroll, enter that total in Step 4(b). This reduces withholding to match your lower expected taxable income — and puts more money in your paycheck every period instead of waiting until April.

Step 4 — Submit and check in mid-year

Give the completed W-4 to your employer's HR or payroll department. Withholding changes on the next paycheck. Then check in around June or July with the IRS Estimator again — if your income has changed (a bonus, a raise, a new freelance contract), recalibrate so you are not surprised in April.

When to re-file a W-4

Any major life event warrants a fresh W-4: getting married or divorced, having a child, buying a home, starting or stopping a second job, or a significant income change. The IRS recommends reviewing it at the start of each year and whenever your situation changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is more tax withheld from my paycheck than I actually owe?

Your employer's withholding tables assume your single paycheck represents your entire year's income at that rate, and that you only take the standard deduction. If you started mid-year, got a raise, have a working spouse, or have deductions that are not reflected on your W-4, the tables will take too much. You settle up at filing — over-withholding comes back as a refund.

Why do I owe taxes if taxes were withheld from every paycheck?

Withholding is only an estimate. If your withholding was set too low — because of a second job, a side income, a working spouse, a mid-year salary increase, or an old W-4 that does not reflect your situation — the running total comes up short by April. The IRS compares total tax owed against total payments made; if payments are less, you owe the difference.

How do I calculate the correct withholding amount?

The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator (irs.gov/W4app) is the most accurate tool. It asks for your income sources, deductions, and credits, then tells you exactly how to fill out Form W-4. You then give the updated W-4 to your employer and withholding adjusts in the next pay cycle.

Is a tax refund free money?

No. A refund means you gave the IRS an interest-free loan for the year. Getting a large refund feels good, but it means your paychecks were smaller than they needed to be all year. A perfectly calibrated W-4 would put that money in your pocket every payday instead of returning it as a lump sum in spring.

Does withholding affect how much total tax I owe?

No. Withholding does not change your tax liability — it only changes the timing of when you pay. Your actual tax is determined by your income, filing status, deductions, and credits, regardless of how much was withheld. Withholding is just the prepayment mechanism; the return is the final accounting.
