The 8 states that tax Social Security — and when

All eight use income thresholds, so most retirees below those levels pay nothing. The rules as of 2026:

  • Colorado — fully exempt at age 65+. Under 65, exempt below $75,000 (single) / $95,000 (joint).
  • Connecticut — exempt below $75,000 (single) / $100,000 (joint); above that, at most 25% of federally-taxable benefits are taxed.
  • Minnesota — full deduction below $86,410 (single) / $110,780 (joint); phases out above.
  • Montana — conforms to federal: if none is federally taxable, none is taxed here.
  • New Mexico — fully exempt at or below $100,000 (single) / $150,000 (joint); taxable above.
  • Rhode Island — exempt once at full retirement age and below $104,200 (single) / $130,250 (joint).
  • Utah — a credit cancels the tax below $54,000 (single) / $90,000 (joint); phases out above.
  • Vermont — fully exempt below $55,000 (single) / $70,000 (joint); phased out by $65,000 / $80,000.

What the rate range doesn't tell you

The rate range is your top marginal rate — the rate on your last dollar. Your effective rate (what you actually pay as a share of income) is always lower, because the standard deduction comes off first and your income fills the low brackets before reaching the top one. Open the calculator to see your personal effective rate for any state.

Local income taxes are also not shown here. They add materially in Indiana, Kentucky, Maryland, Michigan, New York City, Ohio, Oregon (Portland metro), and Pennsylvania.
