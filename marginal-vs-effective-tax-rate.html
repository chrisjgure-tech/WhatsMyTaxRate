Marginal vs. Effective Tax Rate: What's the Difference?

Your marginal rate is the rate on your top dollar. Your effective rate is the average across everything you earned. Most people know the first number and ignore the second — but the second is the one that actually matters for your money.

Most people say they are "in the 22% bracket" and assume that is their tax rate. It is not. Your bracket is your marginal rate — the rate on your last dollar. Your effective rate is the average across all your income, and it is almost always much lower. A single filer earning $100,000 in 2026 has a marginal federal rate of 22% but an effective federal rate of just 13.2%. That gap matters every time you make a financial decision.

What Is the Marginal Tax Rate?

Your marginal tax rate is the rate applied to your next dollar of income. It is the rate of the highest bracket you have reached — nothing more.

The U.S. federal income tax uses seven brackets. They apply to taxable income — your gross income minus the standard deduction (or itemized deductions, if larger). In 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer and $32,200 for married filing jointly.

What Is the Effective Tax Rate?

Your effective tax rate is the average percentage of your total income that goes to federal income tax. The formula is simple:

Effective rate = Total federal income tax ÷ Gross income

If you earn $100,000 and owe $13,170 in federal income tax, your effective rate is $13,170 ÷ $100,000 = 13.2%. Your marginal rate is 22%. The gap is nearly 9 percentage points.

Why is the effective rate so much lower? Because the progressive bracket system taxes your income in layers. The first $12,400 of taxable income is taxed at just 10%. The next $38,000 is taxed at 12%. Only the top slice — the dollars above $50,400 of taxable income — faces the 22% rate.

The effective rate is the honest number. It tells you what share of your total income actually went to the federal government.

How the Bracket System Works

The U.S. federal income tax is progressive. Rates rise as income rises — but only for new income above each threshold. Crossing a higher bracket never raises the tax on income you already earned.

Think of it as filling buckets. The first bucket holds $12,400 of taxable income and is taxed at 10%. When it fills, income spills into the second bucket at 12%. When that fills, the next bucket is at 22%. Each bucket holds only the dollars inside it. Nothing flows backward to re-tax the lower buckets.

This design has one critical implication: you can never take home less money by earning more. A raise that pushes some income into a higher bracket only applies the higher rate to the dollars above the threshold. All the income you already earned keeps its original tax treatment.

The standard deduction shifts the picture further. In 2026, a single filer subtracts $16,100 from gross income before any bracket math starts. That means the first $16,100 of gross income effectively faces zero federal income tax.

According to the Tax Policy Center, the average effective federal income tax rate across all U.S. households was approximately 13.5% in recent years. That is far below the 22%, 24%, and higher marginal rates that dominate headlines about the tax code.

Step-by-Step 2026 Example

Let's walk through the math. You are a single filer with $100,000 of gross income. You take the standard deduction.

Step 1 — Find your taxable income

$100,000 − $16,100 (2026 standard deduction, single) = $83,900 taxable income

Step 2 — Apply the brackets layer by layer

10% on first $12,400 → $1,240.00
12% on $12,401–$50,400 ($38,000) → $4,560.00
22% on $50,401–$83,900 ($33,500) → $7,370.00
Total federal income tax: $13,170

Step 3 — Calculate your rates

Marginal rate: 22% (the bracket your top dollar reached)
Effective rate: $13,170 ÷ $100,000 = 13.2% (the average across all dollars)
Gap: 8.8 percentage points

Why This Distinction Matters for Financial Planning

The gap between marginal and effective rates shows up in real money across several key decisions.

Retirement Contributions

When you contribute to a traditional 401(k) or IRA, you reduce taxable income. Each dollar you contribute saves you your marginal rate in tax today — not your effective rate. If you are in the 22% bracket, a $5,000 contribution cuts your federal tax bill by $1,100. That is the power of a pre-tax deduction: it comes off the top of your income, where the highest rate applies.

According to Vanguard's How America Saves report, the average 401(k) deferral rate was about 7.4% of income in 2023. For a $100,000 earner, that is $7,400 per year — reducing federal taxes by $1,628 at the 22% marginal rate.

Roth vs. Traditional Accounts

Understanding both rates drives the Roth vs. traditional decision. With a traditional account, you save at your marginal rate now but pay at your effective rate in retirement — if your income in retirement is lower than it is today, the tax deferral wins. With a Roth, you pay at your current marginal rate now and owe nothing later.

If your marginal rate is likely to fall in retirement, traditional contributions usually win. If your rate will be the same or higher, Roth is better. Knowing both numbers lets you make that comparison with real math instead of a guess.

Tax-Loss Harvesting and Investment Gains

Investment losses that offset short-term gains save you at your marginal rate on those gains. If your marginal rate is 22%, every $1,000 of harvested losses saves you $220 in federal tax. Knowing your marginal rate — not your average rate — tells you exactly what each dollar of capital gain costs you.

Evaluating a Raise or Side Income

When you earn extra income — a raise, freelance work, a bonus — that new money sits on top of your existing income. It enters the highest bracket you have reached. Your marginal rate tells you how much of each extra dollar you keep. If your marginal rate is 22%, you keep 78 cents of every new dollar (before state tax and FICA). Your effective rate does not change this calculation — what matters is the rate at the margin.

Common Misconceptions

Myth: "I'm in the 22% bracket, so I pay 22% of my income in taxes."

This is the most common mistake. Being in the 22% bracket means your income crossed the 22% threshold — not that all of it is taxed at 22%. The 22% rate applies only to taxable income above $50,400 (single, 2026). Everything below that threshold is still taxed at 10% and 12%. Your effective rate is almost certainly much lower than your marginal rate.

Myth: "A raise pushed me into a higher bracket, so I'll take home less."

This is mathematically impossible in a progressive tax system. Only the dollars above the bracket threshold are taxed at the higher rate. If your raise pushes $5,000 into the 22% bracket, you pay $1,100 more in tax on those $5,000 — but you still net $3,900 more than before. A raise always increases your take-home pay.

Myth: "My bonus is taxed at a higher rate than my salary."

Your bonus is ordinary income. It gets added to your salary and taxed at the same progressive rates. Employers often withhold 22% on bonus payments under the IRS supplemental wage method — but that is just a withholding estimate, not a special tax rate. When you file your return, the actual tax is determined by your full-year income and brackets. If 22% was too much, you get a refund.

Myth: "If I earn just a little more, it might not be worth it."

This fear comes from confusing marginal and effective rates. Even if extra income is taxed at a higher marginal rate, you still keep the after-tax portion. A higher marginal rate slows how fast your after-tax income grows — it does not reverse it. The effective rate on your total income moves up only slightly when a small amount crosses a bracket threshold.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my bonus taxed at my marginal rate?

Not in a special way. Your bonus is ordinary income. It gets added to your salary and run through the same progressive brackets. Employers often withhold a flat 22% on supplemental wages — that is an IRS-approved shortcut, not a separate tax rate for bonuses. When you file your return, if too much was withheld you get a refund. If your marginal rate is above 22%, you may owe a small amount more. Either way, your bonus is not taxed differently than any other income.

How do I lower my effective tax rate?

The most powerful move is to reduce taxable income before brackets apply. A traditional 401(k) or IRA contribution lowers your AGI dollar for dollar. At a 22% marginal rate, a $6,500 IRA contribution saves you $1,430 in federal tax. A Health Savings Account (HSA) is even more efficient — you get a pre-tax contribution, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses. If you have large deductible expenses such as mortgage interest, state taxes, or charitable contributions, itemizing instead of taking the standard deduction can also reduce your taxable income and lower your effective rate.

Does moving into a higher bracket raise tax on income I already earned?

No. Each bracket only taxes the income inside it. In 2026, the 22% bracket starts at $50,400 of taxable income for a single filer. If your taxable income is $53,000, only the $2,600 above $50,400 is taxed at 22%. The first $50,400 stays taxed at 10% and 12%. Earning one more dollar in the 22% bracket costs you 22 cents in federal tax — it does not retroactively change the tax on the dollars below the threshold. You will always take home more money by earning more.

What is the difference between marginal and effective tax rate in plain language?

Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar — the top bracket you reached. Your effective rate is the average across every dollar you earned. For a single filer earning $100,000 in 2026 and taking the standard deduction, the marginal federal rate is 22% but the effective federal rate is about 13.2%. The gap is real and large. It exists because most of your income is taxed at lower rates — 10% and 12% — before the 22% rate kicks in. The effective rate tells you what you actually paid. The marginal rate tells you what the next dollar will cost.
