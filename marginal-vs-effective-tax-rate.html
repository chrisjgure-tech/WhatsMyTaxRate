Marginal vs. Effective Tax Rate: What's the Difference?

Most people quote the bracket they land in and assume that's what they pay. It isn't — not even close. Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar of income. Your effective rate is the average percentage of your total income that actually goes to federal tax. The gap between the two can be ten percentage points or more.

Defining the Terms: Marginal Rate and Effective Rate

The US federal income tax is progressive: the more you earn, the higher the rate on the income above each threshold. That system produces two very different numbers, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes people make when thinking about their taxes.

Marginal Tax Rate

Your marginal tax rate is the rate that applies to your highest — or "last" — dollar of taxable income. If you are a single filer in 2026 with a taxable income of $60,000, the top bracket you reach is 22%. That 22% is your marginal rate. It does not mean you pay 22 cents of every dollar you earn to the federal government. It means you pay 22% only on the slice of income inside that bracket.

Effective Tax Rate

Your effective tax rate is your average rate — your total federal income tax bill divided by your gross income. Because your income fills the lowest brackets first before any of it reaches the top one, your effective rate is always lower than your marginal rate. According to IRS Statistics of Income data, the average effective federal income tax rate across all returns is approximately 13–14%, even though millions of filers carry marginal rates of 22%, 24%, or higher.

The one-sentence rule

Your marginal rate tells you the cost of earning one more dollar. Your effective rate tells you what you actually paid on all your dollars. Both numbers matter — for different questions.

How the Federal Bracket System Works

Think of the federal income tax brackets as a series of buckets. Your income fills the first bucket (taxed at 10%), then overflows into the second (12%), then the third (22%), and so on. The rate on each bucket applies only to the dollars inside that bucket — never to the ones already in the lower buckets.

The difference is nearly nine percentage points. Someone who says "I'm in the 22% bracket" is not paying 22% of their income in federal tax — they're paying about 13.4%.

Why the Distinction Matters for Financial Planning

Understanding which rate to use in which context is the foundation of sound tax planning. Here are the three decisions where the distinction matters most.

1. Evaluating a raise or bonus

Use your marginal rate. The question "how much of this extra money do I keep?" is answered by the marginal rate, because extra income layers on top of everything you already earned. If your marginal rate is 22%, a $10,000 raise nets you roughly $7,800 after federal income tax (before FICA and state taxes).

2. Valuing a deduction

Use your marginal rate. A deduction is worth your marginal rate, because it lowers income from the top down. A $5,000 traditional 401(k) contribution reduces your federal tax bill by roughly $5,000 × your marginal rate — not your effective rate. At 22%, that's $1,100 saved.

3. Comparing your overall tax burden

Use your effective rate. When you want to know what fraction of your income the government actually collected, the effective rate is the right number. It accounts for every bracket below your top one and all your deductions. The calculator on this site shows both your federal effective rate and your all-in effective rate (federal + FICA + state) in a single view.

The Roth vs. traditional 401(k) decision

This distinction sits at the center of one of the most common retirement planning questions. Contributing to a traditional 401(k) saves you your marginal rate today but means withdrawals are taxed at your (unknown future) marginal rate in retirement. A Roth 401(k) costs you your marginal rate today but grows tax-free. If your effective rate today is significantly lower than your marginal rate, a traditional contribution is often the better near-term choice — your deduction is worth the marginal rate, while your overall tax burden (effective rate) is much lower.

Common Misconceptions About Tax Rates

Misconception 1: "I got a raise and now I'm in a higher bracket, so I'll take home less."

False. Moving into a higher bracket only affects the dollars above the threshold — all your previous income is still taxed at the same rates as before. A raise always increases your take-home pay, even if it bumps you into the next bracket.

Misconception 2: "My bonus got destroyed — it was taxed at 40%."

The withholding was high, but the tax is the same. Employers commonly withhold federal tax on bonuses at a flat 22% (for amounts under $1 million) or at an elevated rate using the aggregate method. At filing, the bonus is just ordinary income taxed at your marginal rate. Any over-withholding comes back as part of your refund.

Misconception 3: "The rich pay almost nothing in taxes."

The effective rate for high earners is lower than their marginal rate — but it is not zero. Per IRS Statistics of Income data, the top 1% of earners by adjusted gross income pay an average effective federal income tax rate of approximately 26%, while carrying a top marginal rate of 37%. The gap is real, but so is the tax.

Misconception 4: "My effective rate is the number my employer withholds each paycheck."

Withholding is an estimate, not your actual tax. Your employer uses IRS tables to guess how much to hold back each pay period, assuming each paycheck represents a full year at that rate. If you started mid-year, got a raise, or have significant deductions, the withholding is likely off. Your real effective rate is only known at filing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my bonus taxed at my marginal rate?

Yes — at filing, your bonus is ordinary income stacked on top of your salary and taxed at your marginal rate. However, employers are required to withhold at either a flat 22% (percentage method) or at a higher estimated rate (aggregate method) when they pay the bonus. If that withholding overshoots your actual tax, you get the difference back as a refund.

How do I lower my effective tax rate?

The most reliable method is to reduce taxable income through pre-tax contributions. For 2026:

  • 401(k): up to $23,500 (or $31,000 if age 50+)
  • Traditional IRA: up to $7,000 (or $8,000 if age 50+)
  • HSA: up to $4,300 (self-only) or $8,550 (family)

Each sheltered dollar reduces your taxable income, which can lower both your marginal and effective rate. Use the calculator's shelter panel to model the exact impact at your income level.

Does moving into a higher tax bracket mean I take home less?

No. Only the dollars above the bracket threshold are taxed at the higher rate. Moving into the 22% bracket means you pay 22% on the portion of income that exceeds the 12%-bracket ceiling — not on any dollar below that line. A raise that pushes you into a higher bracket will always increase your take-home pay.

What is a good effective tax rate?

There is no single "good" rate — it depends on income, filing status, and deductions. As a reference: in 2026, a single filer earning $75,000 has a federal effective rate of roughly 12–13%. The IRS Statistics of Income data shows the average effective federal rate across all returns is approximately 13–14%, though this varies widely by income level and filing status.

Is the effective tax rate calculated before or after deductions?

Effective rate is expressed as total federal income tax divided by gross income — so deductions are implicitly accounted for inside the calculation. Because the $16,100 standard deduction (single, 2026) reduces your taxable income before any tax is calculated, someone earning $50,000 pays federal tax on only about $33,900, which is why their effective rate is well below their marginal rate.

What is the difference between effective rate and all-in effective rate?

The federal effective rate includes only federal income tax. The all-in effective rate adds FICA (Social Security and Medicare, totaling 7.65% for employees) and your state income tax. For most W-2 earners, the all-in effective rate is 6–12 percentage points higher than the federal effective rate alone — which is why looking at just the federal bracket can dramatically understate your real tax burden.
