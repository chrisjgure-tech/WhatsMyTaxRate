How to use this table

The US federal income tax is progressive: your income is split into slices, and each slice is taxed at the rate for that bracket only. A higher bracket never raises the tax on money already taxed in a lower one.

Worked example — $80,000 income, Single filer

Start with taxable income after the standard deduction: $80,000 − $16,100 = $63,900.

  • 10% on the first $12,400 = $1,240
  • 12% on $12,401–$50,400 = 12% × $38,000 = $4,560
  • 22% on $50,401–$63,900 = 22% × $13,500 = $2,970

Total federal income tax: $8,770 — an effective rate of 10.96%, not 22%.

The 22% rate applies only to the last $13,500 you earned. The bracket you "land in" is your marginal rate — the rate on your next dollar — not the rate on all your dollars.

Key concepts

Taxable income ≠ gross income

These brackets apply to your taxable income — your gross income minus the standard deduction (or itemized deductions if they're larger). For most W-2 earners, that means subtracting the standard deduction for your filing status before you look at the table above.

Marginal rate vs. effective rate

Your marginal rate is the bracket your last dollar falls into. Your effective rate is your total federal income tax divided by your gross income. Because the lower brackets fill first, your effective rate is always lower than your marginal rate — often by several percentage points.

These brackets cover federal income tax only

FICA (Social Security at 6.2% and Medicare at 1.45%) and any state income tax are calculated separately and on top of what these brackets show. Your all-in effective rate — federal + FICA + state — is higher. Use the calculator on the homepage to see your full picture.
