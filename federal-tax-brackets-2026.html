How to Use This Table

Federal income tax is progressive: your income fills the lowest bracket first, then the next, and so on. Moving into a higher bracket never lowers your take-home pay — only the dollars above each threshold get taxed at the higher rate.

Worked example — Single filer, $75,000 taxable income

Say you're single with $75,000 in taxable income (after the standard deduction). Here's how your tax bill is built up layer by layer:

Bracket Income in bracket Rate Tax owed
10% $11,925 10% $1,192.50
12% $36,550 ($48,475 − $11,925) 12% $4,386.00
22% $26,525 ($75,000 − $48,475) 22% $5,835.50
Total federal income tax $11,414.00

Marginal rate: 22% (the bracket your last dollar of income reaches).
Effective rate: 15.2% ($11,414 ÷ $75,000) — what you actually pay on average.

The difference between the two is why the bracket you land in doesn't equal the rate you pay overall. Use the calculator to see your exact effective rate for any income and filing status.

Key Facts for 2026

  • There are seven brackets: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%.
  • Brackets are adjusted annually for inflation under IRC §1(f). The 2026 figures reflect IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-28.
  • The standard deduction for 2026 is $16,100 (Single/MFS), $32,200 (MFJ), and $23,350 (Head of Household).
  • These brackets apply to ordinary income (wages, salary, tips, interest). Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends are taxed at separate, lower rates (0%, 15%, or 20%).
  • The top 37% bracket applies to taxable income over $626,350 (Single), $751,600 (MFJ), $375,800 (MFS), or $626,350 (HoH).
