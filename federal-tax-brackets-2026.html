How to use this table

Tax brackets are progressive — only the income in each band gets taxed at that band's rate.

Worked example: Single filer with $75,000 taxable income

A common misconception is that earning $75,000 means paying 22% on all of it. That is not how it works. Here is the actual calculation:

  1. 10% on the first $11,925 = $1,192.50
  2. 12% on $11,926 – $48,475 ($36,550) = $4,386.00
  3. 22% on $48,476 – $75,000 ($26,525) = $5,835.50

Total federal income tax: $11,414 — an effective rate of about 15.2%, not 22%.

The 22% bracket describes the rate on your last dollar, not your average. Every dollar below $48,475 is taxed at 10% or 12% regardless of your total income. Moving into a higher bracket never increases the tax on money you already earned in a lower one.

Why your marginal rate ≠ your effective rate

Your marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar of income — the top bracket you reach. Your effective rate is total tax divided by total income, and it is always lower because the lower brackets are filled first. The calculator on this site shows you both, so you can see the difference for your actual income.
